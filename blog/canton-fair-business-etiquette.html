Canton Fair Business Etiquette: Dos and Don'ts

Updated 12 July 2026 · 5 min read · By Nexera Sourcing and Exim

Quick answer: At the Canton Fair, be punctual, exchange business cards with both hands, stay polite and patient, and focus on building a relationship. Small courtesies and clear communication go a long way in Chinese business culture.

Simple courtesies matter

  • Be on time for booked meetings, and message ahead if you are running even a few minutes late.
  • Offer and receive business cards with both hands, and take a moment to look at a card before putting it away.
  • Be polite and patient, even when negotiating firmly on price or terms.
  • Dress smartly and professionally, since first impressions matter more than casual attire suggests in this setting.

Build the relationship

Chinese business culture places heavy weight on trust and long term relationships, often summarised locally as guanxi. A respectful, friendly approach to a supplier frequently unlocks better terms, faster responses and more flexibility on minimum order quantities than an aggressive, purely transactional approach ever will. Suppliers remember buyers who treat them as partners rather than as one time vendors, and this pays off across every reorder that follows the fair.

Communicate clearly

Confirm key points in writing after every meeting, including price, specifications, quantities and delivery timelines, so both sides have the same understanding. Use an interpreter when needed, not because English is uncommon at major booths, but because nuanced details around quality standards or payment terms are exactly where misunderstandings happen. A short written summary emailed the same evening also gives the supplier a clear record to work from.

Negotiating without damaging the relationship

Firm negotiation is entirely normal and expected at the Canton Fair, and suppliers anticipate that buyers will compare quotes and push on price. The distinction that matters is between firm and disrespectful. Stay courteous while holding your position, avoid raising your voice or making the negotiation personal, and give the supplier room to save face if you need to reject a quote. A polite but clear no, paired with a reason, usually gets a better counter offer than pressure tactics, and preserves the relationship for future orders.

Dos and don'ts at the booth

Do greet the booth staff before diving into questions, do ask permission before photographing products or production samples, and do take a seat if one is offered rather than standing over the table. Don't criticise a competitor's product loudly while at a booth, don't make promises about order size you are not confident you can keep, and don't rush a meeting so much that it feels transactional rather than relationship building. These small habits compound into how a supplier prioritises you among the dozens of buyers they see each phase.

Etiquette after the fair

Good etiquette does not end when you leave the booth. Follow up within a few days while the conversation is still fresh, thank the supplier for their time, and keep your tone consistent with how you presented yourself in person. Buyers who go silent for weeks after promising a follow up damage their credibility for future dealings, while a prompt, professional follow up message reinforces the trust built at the fair. Etiquette with an interpreter in the room

When using an interpreter, speak directly to the supplier rather than to the interpreter, keep sentences short and clear to make translation easier, and pause regularly rather than speaking in long uninterrupted blocks. A good interpreter also picks up on cultural nuance in the supplier's tone that a literal translation alone would miss, so it is worth briefly checking in with them after a meeting for their read on how it actually went.

Gift giving and hospitality norms

Small gifts, such as a token item representing India or your company, are appreciated but never expected, and modest hospitality such as accepting tea or a meal invitation is a normal part of relationship building rather than an obligation to reciprocate immediately. Accept graciously, and if you wish to reciprocate, a simple thank you message afterward is sufficient and well received.

Frequently asked questions

What etiquette should I follow at the Canton Fair?

Be punctual, exchange cards respectfully, stay polite, dress smartly and focus on building the relationship.

Do I need an interpreter for etiquette reasons?

An interpreter helps you communicate clearly and shows you take the relationship seriously.

Is haggling aggressively considered rude in China?

Firm negotiation is normal and expected, but aggressive or disrespectful haggling can damage the relationship. Stay firm on price while remaining courteous.

What small gestures make a good impression with Chinese suppliers?

Remembering names, following up promptly after the fair, and showing genuine interest in their factory and products all build goodwill beyond the meeting itself.
